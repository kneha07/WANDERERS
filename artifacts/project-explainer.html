WANDERERS

WANDERERS plays spot-the-difference with the sky.

Most stars look like they stay still. A few things slowly move. We look at pictures of the sky from many years and find the dot that moved.

Then we look at the dot's colours, to guess what it is. It is like telling a lemon from an orange by its colour.

Our big rule: we say "maybe", never "we found it!"

The idea in 3 steps

How do you find a thing that moves?

  • 1. Take pictures, many years apart

    Space telescopes took pictures of the same bit of sky in 2010, every 6 months until 2024, then in 2025 and 2026.

  • 2. Flip between them, like a flipbook

    Stars far away stay in the same place. A thing that is close to us seems to walk across the picture.

  • 3. Check the list of known stars

    Scientists already have a big list of stars. If the moving dot is not on the list, it is a mystery. We call it a "candidate".

Grown-up words: "moving" = proper motion. The big star list = ESA Gaia DR3. The circles in the picture are "Gaia ghosts". A man found Pluto in 1930 with this same flip trick.

Why we made it

What we want to do

  • Do all six homework tasks

    NASA gave every team six tasks. WANDERERS does all six. You can try each one on the website.

  • Find what moves

    Spot the dots that walk across the sky over 16 years.

  • Tell what it is made of

    A special telescope sees 102 colours. Our eyes cannot see them. Water and other stuff leave their own pattern in those colours.

  • Always show why

    When the app makes a guess, it shows the clues. "3 of 3 clues agree" means a good guess.

  • Never fib

    If we are not sure, we say "maybe". We also say what the pictures cannot show.

  • Use only real pictures

    Every picture comes from NASA or other real telescopes. Nothing is made up.

A guessing game

How it guesses what a dot is

The app asks questions in this order, like the game "20 Questions". The first "yes" gives the answer.

Is it cold, a bit red, and moving?A "failed star" (brown dwarf)
Does it get brighter and dimmer?A blinking star
Is it moving, and on the big star list?A close star we already know
Is it moving, and in a science book?A close thing someone already found
Is it moving, and on no list at all?A mystery! We say "maybe"
Does the list say it blinks, but it was calm?A blinking star, resting today
None of these?A normal star that stays still

Then it counts the clues that agree. 3 or more clues: a strong guess. 2 clues: a medium guess. 0 or 1 clue: a weak guess. Code: pipeline/evidence.py.

NASA's homework

The six tasks

#The taskWhat WANDERERS does
1Keep pictures freshA robot checks every Monday for new telescope pictures. The page shows the date of the newest ones.
2Mix six camerasThe telescope has six cameras. You can look at one at a time, or mix all six to see faint things better.
3Say if a picture is goodEach picture gets a label: good, careful, or poor. It also says why, for example "too bright here".
4Let people voteYou can vote: moving, blinking, still, a mistake, or not sure. When 3 or more people mostly agree, the page shows it.
5Make a "best of" listAll the mystery dots go in one list. The best ones are at the top. You can sort it and download it.
6Search the whole skyThe sky is cut into 12,288 puzzle pieces. A robot checks every piece, one by one, all by itself.

We tell the truth

Things it cannot do yet

  • Very bright stars are too bright for the camera, like a photo of the Sun. We skip those parts and say so.
  • The colour pattern shows the shape, but not exact amounts.
  • On the public website you can see only the ready-made bits of sky. A new bit takes about 15 minutes on your own computer.
  • The robot finds its own practice star in 8 of 9 tests. It still misses WISE 0855.
  • An empty list means "we found nothing". It does not mean "nothing is there".
